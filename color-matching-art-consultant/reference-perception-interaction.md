## §1. The core premise

- Color is the most **relative** medium in art: "color is almost never seen as it really is." What a color *is* physically and what it *does* perceptually are two different things (Albers — opening premise / relativity of color).
- Practical consequence: a swatch judged in isolation tells you little about how it will read *in place*. Always evaluate a color against its actual neighbors, ground, and area, not on the palette.
- This is the layer that answers "why does it look different here?" — separate it explicitly from paint-mixture facts (a physical change to the pigment) and from historical association. An interaction effect means the pigment did not change; its *surround* changed what the eye reports.

## §2. Simultaneous contrast (the engine)

- A color pushes its neighbors toward its own **complement**. Put a color on a ground and the ground induces its opposite into the color: the same gray reads warmer on a cool ground and cooler on a warm ground; lighter on a dark ground, darker on a light one (Albers — ch. on simultaneous contrast).
- Every interaction effect below is a special case of this. When a chosen color "goes wrong" only in a particular spot, suspect the neighbor is inducing its complement.
- Direction of the shift: toward the complement of the **dominant surrounding color**, and toward the opposite in value, hue, and chroma at once.

## §3. Afterimage / successive contrast

- Stare at a strong color, then look at a white field: you see its complement as a ghost image. The eye generates the opposite it was "missing" (Albers — ch. on afterimage / reversed afterimage).
- This is the perceptual mechanism behind simultaneous contrast, played out over time instead of across space. It's why complementary pairs feel "resolved" and why a fatigued eye drifts — long looking at a saturated ground biases every subsequent judgment.
- Use it to explain shadow color to painters: an afterimage/induced complement is why a shadow cast on a yellow-lit surface can read violet (cross-ref the "violet" note in `reference-color-histories.md` → Purples).

## §4. One color looks like two

- A single color placed on two different grounds can be made to look like **two different colors** — Albers's central classroom demonstration (Albers — "one color appears as two").
- Diagnostic use: if two areas that are *the same pigment* look different in the composition, that is expected, not a mistake. The difference is manufactured by their differing surrounds.
- Lever: to make them read alike, equalize the grounds behind them, or insert the same buffer color around each. To exaggerate the difference deliberately, widen the contrast between the two grounds.

## §5. Two colors look like one

- The reverse trick: two genuinely different colors can be pushed to look **alike** by choosing grounds that shift each toward the other (Albers — "two different colors look alike / as one").
- Practical payoff: you can make a near-match "pass" in context without physically matching the pigments — a **perceptual match** rather than an exact match. Name it as such (the parent skill's match-type vocabulary).
- Caution: the illusion is context-locked. Move either color to a new ground, or change the light, and the match breaks. Don't promise it will hold outside the tested setting.

## §6. Value / lightness deception

- Value (lightness) is the hardest dimension to judge because color interferes with it. Identical grays look unequal on different grounds; a light color on a dark ground can be mistaken for a lighter color than it is (Albers — light intensity / lightness studies).
- Two colors of very different hue can share the **same value**, and the eye struggles to see it. This is exactly why the parent skill checks value *first* and often recommends a grayscale/squint test — squinting collapses hue and exposes the value structure.
- Failure this predicts: a palette that "looks flat" despite varied hues is usually collapsed in value, not lacking in color. Fix by spreading values, not by adding more hues.

## §7. Vibrating and vanishing boundaries

- **Vibrating boundary:** two high-chroma colors of *similar value* — especially near-complements — set side by side make the shared edge flicker and buzz; the boundary won't sit still (Albers — ch. on vibrating boundaries).
- **Vanishing boundary:** two colors of the *same value* but different hue lose their edge; the boundary dissolves and the shapes bleed together (Albers — ch. on vanishing boundaries).
- These are opposite failure modes of the same variable — the value relationship at the edge:
  - Buzzing edge you didn't want → **lower one color's chroma**, shift their values apart, or separate them with a thin neutral/line.
  - Dissolving edge you wanted crisp → the two are **equal in value**; separate them by value, not by hue. Two hues at the same value will never hold a hard edge.

## §8. Quantity, proportion, and film vs. surface color

- **Quantity changes appearance.** The same color reads more intense in a small patch and calmer/lighter in a large field; changing the *amount* of a color changes how strong it looks and how it balances against its neighbors (Albers — studies on quantity/proportion). This is the perceptual basis for using strong color as a small accent and muted color across large areas (and it echoes Munsell's area-balance — see `reference-notation-measurement.md`).
- **Film vs. surface color.** A color can appear as a thin, weightless *film* (like a glow, hard to locate in space) or as a *surface* attached to an object. The same pigment can be pushed toward either reading by how it's bounded and surrounded — relevant when a passage should read as light/atmosphere rather than as a painted object (Albers — film color / color as boundary).

## §9. Transparency and the illusion of space

- Where two colors overlap, a well-chosen third color reads as their **transparent** blend — the eye infers layering that isn't physically there (Albers — ch. on the illusion of transparence / space). The "correct" middle color is a specific mixture; the wrong one kills the illusion and the overlap reads as an opaque third shape.
- Boundaries and overlaps also drive apparent **space**: warmer/lighter/higher-chroma passages tend to come forward, cooler/darker/grayer ones recede — but context can reverse this, so treat advance/recede as a tendency the surround can override, not a fixed law (Albers — space illusion; cross-ref warm-advance/cool-recede in `reference-harmony-palette.md`).

## §10. Composition diagnostics (the fast table)

Use when a user reports "it looks wrong" but the pigments are fine — i.e., the problem is interaction, not mixture.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Same color looks different in two spots | Different grounds inducing different complements (§4) | Equalize the surrounds, or accept it as intentional |
| An accent disappears / won't pop | Its value or chroma is too close to the ground; or too much competing chroma nearby | Raise value **or** chroma contrast against the ground; lower competing chroma |
| Two areas that should match don't | Simultaneous contrast (§2) pulling each toward a different complement | Buffer both with the same neighbor, or match the grounds |
| Edge buzzes / vibrates | Near-complements at equal value, high chroma (§7) | Lower one chroma, shift values apart, or insert a neutral divider |
| Edge dissolves where you wanted separation | Two hues at the **same value** (§7) | Separate by value, not hue |
| A hue reads muddy in place but clean on the swatch | Surround is inducing its complement into it (§2) | Change the neighbor/ground, not the pigment |
| Palette looks flat despite many hues | Value collapse (§6) | Spread values; don't add more colors |
| Strong color feels overwhelming | Too large an area at full chroma (§8) | Shrink it to an accent, or lower its chroma over the large field |
